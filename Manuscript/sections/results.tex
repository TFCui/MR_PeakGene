\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
\subsection{A Mendelian randomization framework for causal enhancer--gene inference}
\label{res:analysis-set}

We propose a Mendelian randomization (MR) framework to estimate the causal effect of chromatin accessibility at a candidate peak on the gene expression within a given cell type (Fig.~1A). Marginal peak--gene associations measure the Pearson or Spearman correlation between accessibility and expression. This association equals the causal effect only if no other factor, such as cell identity, transcription factor activity, or differences between donors, affects both; otherwise, it also contains a confounding bias that can mask, inflate, or even reverse the causal effect (\nameref{S1_Text}, Section~1). MR addresses this problem by using genetic variants as instrumental variables. Because alleles are randomly allocated at meiosis, a variant that affects accessibility is expected to be independent of confounders. If the variant also affects the gene expression only through the peak accessibility, the ratio of its association with expression to its association with accessibility equals the causal effect (\nameref{S1_Text}, Section~1). For each peak--gene pair, we use caQTL and eQTL summary statistics from the same cell type for \(J\) \textit{cis} SNPs strongly associated with peak accessibility (nominal caQTL \(P<10^{-8}\); \nameref{sec:data-preprocessing}). Let \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=(\hat{\gamma}_1,\ldots,\hat{\gamma}_J)^{\mathsf T}\) and \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}=(\hat{\Gamma}_1,\ldots,\hat{\Gamma}_J)^{\mathsf T}\) denote the estimated SNP associations with peak accessibility and gene expression, respectively.

\noindent Causal IVW and causal Egger regress the eQTL estimates on the caQTL estimates using the model:
\[
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}
= \alpha_0\boldsymbol{1}_J + \beta\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
  + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon},
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
\sim \mathcal{N}_J(\boldsymbol{0},\phi\boldsymbol{\Sigma}).
\]
\noindent Here, \(\beta\) represents the causal effect of peak accessibility on gene expression under the MR assumptions (\nameref{S1_Text}, Section~2), and \(\alpha_0\) represents the weighted average pleiotropic effect, that is, the effect of the SNPs on expression that is not mediated by the peak. Causal IVW fixes \(\alpha_0=0\) and is consistent when all instruments are valid or pleiotropy is balanced, that is, when the pleiotropic effects average to zero and are unrelated to instrument strength. Causal Egger estimates \(\alpha_0\) and therefore also allows for directional pleiotropy; its slope remains consistent under the Instrument Strength Independent of Direct Effect (InSIDE) assumption (\nameref{S1_Text}, Section~3). Because the Egger intercept depends on which allele is coded as the effect allele, we orient all SNPs to the accessibility-increasing allele (\nameref{sec:orientation}). To increase power, both methods use correlated \textit{cis} SNPs as instruments and fit the model by generalized least squares, with \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) built from the eQTL standard errors and the LD among SNPs (\nameref{sec:gls-ivw}; \nameref{S1_Text}, Sections~7 and 9). Because some SNPs may be pleiotropic, their ratio estimates can be more heterogeneous than expected from sampling error alone; the dispersion parameter \(\phi\) accounts for this overdispersion through a multiplicative random-effects model (\nameref{S1_Text}, Section~8).

\noindent Both regressions assume no measurement error (NOME) in the caQTL estimates \(\hat{\gamma}_j\). Because the caQTL and eQTL estimates come from largely the same CIMA donors, a violation of NOME biases the estimates toward the marginal association; this bias is approximately inversely proportional to instrument strength and is small for causal IVW with our caQTL threshold of \(P<10^{-8}\), although causal Egger is more sensitive \cite{bowden2016nome} (\nameref{S1_Text}, Sections~3 and 4). Consistent with this, the correlation between the caQTL and eQTL \(z\)-scores of null SNPs, which estimates the error correlation, was small in all three cell types (\nameref{sec:overlap}; \nameref{S1_Fig}).

\noindent Causal SMR follows the summary-data-based MR approach \cite{zhu2016smr} and uses a single instrument, the lead SNP with the smallest caQTL \(P\) value for the peak. Denoting this SNP by \(*\), the causal effect is estimated by the Wald ratio \(\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{SMR}}=\hat{\Gamma}_*/\hat{\gamma}_*\). Unlike causal IVW and causal Egger, causal SMR requires neither an LD matrix nor a model for pleiotropy across instruments. Its test statistic accounts for the sampling errors of both the caQTL and eQTL estimates and is large only when the lead SNP is strongly associated with both accessibility and expression (\nameref{sec:smr}). Causal joint combines the \(P\) values of the three tests with an equally weighted Cauchy combination test, whose \(P\) value remains accurate when the combined \(P\) values are correlated, as they are here because the three tests share instruments \cite{liu2020cauchy} (\nameref{sec:cct}).

\noindent We applied the framework to cell-type-specific caQTL and eQTL summary statistics from the Chinese Immune Multi-Omics Atlas (CIMA), which profiled circulating immune cells from 428 healthy Chinese adults using scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq \cite{yin2026cima}.

\noindent For comparison, we calculated two marginal measures of peak--gene association (Fig.~1A), which follow the correlation-based approach of tools such as Signac, ArchR, and scMEGA \cite{stuart2021signac,granja2021archr,li2023scmega}. Marginal multiomics calculates the Spearman correlation between peak accessibility and gene expression across 80 weighted nearest-neighbor (WNN) metacells from an external peripheral blood mononuclear cell multiome dataset \cite{tenx2020pbmc10k,jiang2022tripod}. Because these metacells span multiple immune cell types, the correlation captures co-variation of accessibility and expression across cell types and states, and a pair tested in different cell types has the same estimate (\nameref{sec:wnn-correlations}). Marginal pseudobulk calculates the same correlation across CIMA donors within each cell type, using donor-level pseudobulk profiles normalized to log-transformed counts per million without covariate adjustment (\nameref{sec:pseudobulk-correlations}); it therefore captures co-variation across individuals within a single cell type. Both measures estimate the marginal association between accessibility and expression and are therefore subject to the confounding described above (\nameref{S1_Text}, Fig A).

\subsection{Concordance and discordance between causal effects and marginal associations}
\label{res:examples}

Within each cell type, we applied all six methods to the same set of candidate peak--gene pairs (40,087 in Bn cells, 85,527 in CD4 T cells, and 50,931 in CD8 T cells; \nameref{S3_Table}). At a threshold of 0.1 on the within-gene BH-adjusted \(P\) value (\(\q\); \nameref{sec:terminology}), the four MR-based tests identified between 7,265 (causal Egger) and 10,597 (causal joint) links among the CD4 T cell candidate pairs, far fewer than the 37,181 links identified by marginal multiomics and the 17,181 identified by marginal pseudobulk (Fig~\ref{fig:overview}B). That marginal multiomics detected so many more links, most of them (24,632) by no other method, may partly reflect confounding by cell type: because the metacells span several immune cell types, differences between cell types can induce correlations between accessibility and expression even in the absence of direct regulation (\nameref{S1_Text}, Section~1). Conversely, 4,749 CD4 T cell links were significant for at least one of causal IVW, causal Egger, and causal SMR but for neither marginal method; we refer to these as MR-only links (\nameref{sec:terminology}). The largest such combination comprised 870 links detected jointly by causal SMR, causal IVW, and causal joint. Bn cells and CD8 T cells showed the same pattern (\nameref{S3_Fig}). Among the 14,890 candidate pairs tested in all three cell types, 91\% of marginal multiomics links were significant in all three cell types, compared with 9--22\% for the four MR-based tests and 13\% for marginal pseudobulk (\nameref{S4_Fig}).

\noindent We next examined whether the methods agreed on the direction of the association for the 1,259 CD4 T cell links that were significant for all six methods (Fig~\ref{fig:overview}C). The causal IVW, causal Egger, and causal SMR estimates agreed in sign for 1,258 of these links, indicating that the direction of the estimated effect was consistent across different MR methods. Disagreement with the MR estimates, by contrast, was concentrated in the metacell correlation. The causal IVW estimate agreed in sign with both marginal correlations for 918 links (72.9\%); for 304 links, only the metacell correlation had the opposite sign, compared with 17 links for which only the pseudobulk correlation did, and for the remaining 20 links both correlations had the opposite sign. In total, the metacell correlation disagreed in sign with the causal IVW estimate for 25.7\% of these links, whereas the pseudobulk correlation did so for only 2.9\%; the corresponding proportions were 24.1\% and 1.1\% in Bn cells and 24.8\% and 2.1\% in CD8 T cells (\nameref{S3_Fig}).

\noindent This asymmetry was not confined to links significant for all six methods. Across all tested pairs, the causal IVW estimates were only modestly correlated with the pseudobulk correlations (Spearman \(r=0.24\)--\(0.30\) across the three cell types) and were essentially uncorrelated with the metacell correlations (\(r=0.03\)--\(0.04\)) (\nameref{S6_Fig}). The two marginal measures were likewise nearly uncorrelated with each other (\(r=0.04\)--\(0.06\)). The MR estimates, in contrast, agreed closely with one another: causal IVW was correlated with causal SMR at \(r=0.86\)--\(0.87\) and with causal Egger at \(r=0.55\)--\(0.56\). Signed \(-\log_{10}P\) values showed the same pattern (\nameref{S6_Fig}). Across the genome, therefore, the MR estimates were largely unrelated to the marginal associations, and particularly to those measured across metacells.

\noindent Four examples illustrate these patterns (Fig~\ref{fig:examples}). For \textit{SIDT2} and the peak at chr11:117185715--117186216 in Bn cells, all six tests were significant (Fig~\ref{fig:examples}A). The caQTL and eQTL association profiles of the 194 instruments closely mirrored each other, the SNP effects on expression were proportional to those on accessibility, and the three MR estimates and both marginal correlations were positive. For \textit{RPL11} and the peak at chr1:23691494--23691995 in CD4 T cells, the MR tests and the metacell correlation were also significant, but the correlation across donors was weak and did not reach significance (Fig~\ref{fig:examples}B). The \textit{ERAP2} example illustrates a difference in direction: for the peak at chr5:96885894--96886395 in CD4 T cells, all three MR estimates were negative, as was the correlation across donors (\(\rho=-0.641\), \(P=2.0\times10^{-44}\)), whereas the correlation across metacells was positive (\(\rho=0.375\), \(P=6.1\times10^{-4}\)) (Fig~\ref{fig:examples}C). Finally, for \textit{ARPC1B} and the peak at chr7:99376204--99376705 in CD4 T cells, all four MR-based tests were significant and all three MR estimates were positive, yet neither marginal correlation reached significance (Fig~\ref{fig:examples}D).

\noindent These examples, together with the asymmetry in Fig~\ref{fig:overview}C, are consistent with the confounding of marginal associations described in \nameref{S1_Text}, Section~1. Marginal correlations can reflect shared variation in accessibility and expression across cell types or states \cite{su2025scmultimap,calderon2019landscape}. If both peak accessibility and gene expression are higher in one cell type than in another, for example, pooling metacells across these types can yield a positive correlation even when the association within a given cell type is weak or negative \cite{su2025scmultimap}. Within a cell type, differences in immune activation across donors could likewise influence both measurements and contribute to the pseudobulk correlation \cite{calderon2019landscape}. Conversely, a confounder whose contribution offsets the causal effect can mask it in a marginal correlation (\nameref{S1_Text}, Section~1), which may explain why the \textit{RPL11} link was not detected across donors and the \textit{ARPC1B} link was detected by neither marginal method despite strong MR evidence. Thus, a marginal correlation alone cannot establish that accessibility at a candidate enhancer causally affects gene expression. By using genetic variants as instruments, MR methods provide complementary evidence about causal effects and can identify candidate regulatory links even when marginal correlations are weak, as illustrated by \textit{ARPC1B} (Fig~\ref{fig:examples}D).

\subsection{Orthogonal regulatory evidence for MR-inferred enhancer--gene links}

\noindent To extend the comparison beyond individual loci, we compared the genome-wide significance patterns of the causal joint test and the two marginal analyses in CD4 T cells (Fig~\ref{fig:go}A; all six methods and all three cell types are shown in \nameref{S7_Fig}). The three tracks differed in both the strength and the location of their strongest signals. The causal joint signals were concentrated in a limited number of pairs, several of which exceeded \(-\log_{10}P=150\), whereas the marginal multiomics signals were more numerous but weaker, with no pair exceeding \(-\log_{10}P=35\). The genes with the strongest marginal multiomics signals, such as \textit{LEF1}, \textit{CD7}, \textit{ETS1}, and \textit{IKZF3}, were absent from the top causal joint signals, whereas causal joint and marginal pseudobulk shared several of their top genes, including \textit{LCLAT1}, \textit{TMEM8A}, \textit{RNASET2}, and \textit{CBR3}. Moreover, some pairs with strong MR evidence showed little evidence of association in either marginal analysis, as illustrated by \textit{ARPC1B}. We therefore characterized MR-only links further, beginning with the functions of the genes involved.

\noindent Pooling MR-only links across Bn cells, CD4 T cells, and CD8 T cells yielded 4,509 unique genes, which we tested for Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment using DAVID \cite{sherman2022david} (see Methods; Fig~\ref{fig:go}B). Three themes emerged. The first was transcriptional regulation: DNA-templated transcription was the most significantly enriched biological process (689 genes, 1.36-fold enrichment, BH-adjusted \(P=5.20\times10^{-18}\)), and regulation of transcription by RNA polymerase II was also enriched (415 genes, 1.26-fold, BH-adjusted \(P=4.70\times10^{-5}\)). The second was RNA processing, which spanned both GO categories: RNA splicing was enriched among biological processes (84 genes, 1.69-fold, BH-adjusted \(P=3.39\times10^{-4}\)), as were RNA binding and mRNA binding among molecular functions, with \textit{SRSF7} and \textit{DDX5} annotated to all three terms. RNA processing is dynamically regulated in naive lymphocytes: human naive B cells in secondary lymphoid tissue show less intron retention than those in peripheral blood, even before activation \cite{ogrady2022splicing}, and changes in alternative splicing have been observed within six hours of stimulating naive CD4 T cells, with similar overall patterns reported in CD8 T cells \cite{zisman2025splicing}. The third theme was protein ubiquitination, which was enriched both as a biological process (172 genes, 1.50-fold, BH-adjusted \(P=1.25\times10^{-5}\)) and through the corresponding molecular function, ubiquitin-protein transferase activity (92 genes, 1.66-fold, BH-adjusted \(P=1.55\times10^{-4}\)); both terms included \textit{UBE2N}, which encodes the ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme Ubc13 \cite{yamamoto2006ubc13}. Because enrichment was assessed against a genome-wide background and the fold enrichments were modest (1.26--1.69 for these terms), these results describe the functions represented among genes in MR-only links rather than functions unique to links detected by MR.

\noindent We next assessed whether significant links overlapped independent regulatory annotations more often than candidate pairs did, using six external references: GTEx whole-blood eQTLs, EnhancerAtlas, FANTOM5, 4DGenome, H3K27ac HiChIP, and promoter capture Hi-C (see Methods). For each method, cell type, and reference, we compared the proportion of significant links supported by the reference with the corresponding proportion among candidate pairs using a pooled hypergeometric test. All six methods were enriched for every reference in every cell type (\nameref{S1_Table}). Because these \(P\) values also depend on the number of links, which was largest for marginal multiomics, we compared the methods by fold enrichment: causal SMR had the largest fold enrichment in 17 of the 18 combinations of cell type and reference, whereas marginal multiomics had the smallest in all 18 (\nameref{S2_Table}).

\noindent We then asked whether MR-only links, which by definition lack marginal support, also overlapped these references. For each gene, we took the union of its MR-only peaks across the three cell types and, separately, the union of its candidate peaks. Because reference coverage varied among genes, we divided genes into eight equally sized groups according to the proportion of their candidate peaks overlapping the gene's reference regions and, within each group, compared this pooled background proportion with the pooled overlap proportion for MR-only peaks. For H3K27ac HiChIP, the overlap proportion for MR-only peaks exceeded the background proportion in all eight groups, placing every group above the diagonal that marks no enrichment (Fig~\ref{fig:go}C). Overall, MR-only peaks were 3.28 times as likely as candidate peaks to overlap a HiChIP contact region assigned to the same gene (pooled hypergeometric \(P=6.4\times10^{-620}\)). The other five references showed similar patterns, with fold enrichments of 1.89--2.87 (\nameref{S5_Fig}). Thus, MR identified candidate regulatory links with independent support even when neither marginal correlation reached significance.

\noindent Peak--gene links identified by MR also showed stronger enrichment in the CIMA gene regulatory network than those identified by the two marginal methods. To quantify this enrichment, we extracted unique region--gene pairs from the enhancer-driven regulons (eRegulons) that CIMA inferred using SCENIC+ \cite{yin2026cima}. For each method and cell type, we calculated an odds ratio comparing the odds of eRegulon membership among significant peak--gene pairs (\(\q<0.05\)) with the corresponding odds among the remaining tested pairs (Fisher's exact test, all \(P<10^{-30}\); see Methods). Although the marginal methods identified more links (Fig~\ref{fig:overview}B; \nameref{S3_Fig}), the MR methods generally yielded higher odds ratios, particularly for the high-quality eRegulon subset (Fig~\ref{fig:go}D; \nameref{S8_Fig}). These results provide additional support for the regulatory relevance of MR-identified peak--gene links.

\noindent Because eRegulons connect regulatory regions to target genes through transcription factors, we next characterized the transcription factor motifs enriched in the peaks identified by each method (see Methods). For each cell type and method, the peaks of links with \(\q<0.1\) formed the foreground set, and the background consisted of 50,000 candidate peaks matched for GC content and chromatin accessibility. We focused on MR-specific motifs, defined as motifs enriched for at least one of causal IVW, causal Egger, and causal SMR but for neither marginal method. We identified 47 such motifs (23 in Bn cells, 14 in CD4 T cells, and 10 in CD8 T cells), of which Table~\ref{tab:motif} lists the two most significant in each cell type. All six motifs in Table~\ref{tab:motif} were enriched for causal Egger, and four of them (FOSL2::JUND, MNT, POU3F1, and TEF) were enriched only for causal Egger.

\noindent In CD4 T cells, the MNT motif showed the strongest enrichment (motif-level adjusted \(P=0.003\) and 1.34-fold enrichment for causal Egger); loss of MNT has been reported in malignant CD4\(^+\) T cells from patients with S\'{e}zary syndrome~\cite{vermeer2008mnt}. In Bn cells, the ATF3 motif showed the strongest enrichment, and peaks selected by causal Egger were also enriched for the FOSL2::JUND motif. JunD, one of the two AP-1 components of this motif, is upregulated in B cells from patients with SLE~\cite{wu2024jund}. The remaining motifs were CENPB in CD4 T cells and POU3F1 and TEF in CD8 T cells, whose reported disease associations are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:motif}. Together, these analyses show that MR-supported links, including many without marginal support, are enriched for independent regulatory annotations and for the CIMA regulatory network.

\subsection{Heritability enrichment and candidate target genes for immune-mediated diseases}

To assess the disease relevance of MR-supported peak--gene links, we evaluated heritability enrichment for seven immune-mediated diseases: ankylosing spondylitis (AS), Crohn's disease (CD), inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), rheumatoid arthritis (RA), systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), multiple sclerosis (MS), and type 1 diabetes (T1D) (\nameref{S4_Table}). We hypothesized that peaks involved in MR-only links would capture disease-relevant regulatory signals missed by marginal correlation analyses. We therefore pooled these peaks across the three cell types and estimated their heritability enrichment using stratified LD score regression (S-LDSC) \cite{finucane2015sldsc}, controlling for 97 baseline annotations, with all candidate peaks as a comparison (see Methods). All candidate peaks were themselves enriched for the heritability of every disease except AS (\(P<10^{-7}\); Fig~\ref{fig:disease}A). Peaks involved in MR-only links had higher point estimates of heritability enrichment than all candidate peaks for all seven diseases (for example, 20.0 versus 6.36 for CD and 19.7 versus 4.64 for T1D), with nominal enrichment \(P<0.1\) for CD, IBD, MS, and T1D (Fig~\ref{fig:disease}A). The weaker statistical evidence for MR-only peaks likely reflects their small size: they covered 0.25\% of SNPs, compared with 15.1\% for all candidate peaks. Together, these results provide nominal evidence that peaks in MR-only links, which marginal correlation analyses do not prioritize, are enriched for the heritability of several immune-mediated diseases.

\noindent We next present three examples illustrating how MR-supported links can help identify candidate target genes and cellular contexts for disease-associated variants. Although GWAS have identified many loci associated with immune-mediated diseases, the mechanisms through which these loci act and the relevant cell types often remain unclear \cite{gallagher2018postgwas}. Among the 24 strong MR-only links whose peaks contained a genome-wide significant variant for at least one of the seven diseases (see Methods), we selected one example from each cell type with supporting regulatory annotations and previous literature (Fig~\ref{fig:disease}B). In the first example, the MR methods linked the peak at chr5:96,896,259--96,896,760 (hg38), which contains the CD-associated variant rs2549785, to \textit{ERAP1} in Bn cells. The 5q15 region harboring \textit{ERAP1} is a known CD-associated locus \cite{franke2010cd}. \textit{ERAP1} encodes an endoplasmic reticulum aminopeptidase that trims peptides for presentation by MHC class I molecules \cite{hattori2013erap}, and immune-mediated disease-associated variants at 5q15 affect its transcript and protein expression \cite{hanson2018erap}; in mice with DSS-induced colitis, ERAP1 deficiency increased colonic proinflammatory cytokine levels \cite{blake2022erap1}. Independent regulatory annotations also support the peak--gene link: the peak overlaps an enhancer predicted to target \textit{ERAP1} by EnhancerAtlas \cite{gao2020enhanceratlas}, and naive B-cell H3K27ac HiChIP data identified a contact between the peak and the \textit{ERAP1} promoter ($Q=8.4\times10^{-3}$) \cite{chandra2021pieqtl}. RegulomeDB further annotates rs2549785 as overlapping a transcription factor binding site or an accessible chromatin region \cite{boyle2012regulomedb,dong2023regulomedb}. In our analysis, higher accessibility of this peak was estimated to decrease \textit{ERAP1} expression in Bn cells (causal IVW \(\hat{\beta}=-0.36\), \(\q=8.0\times10^{-6}\)), whereas neither marginal method detected the link (\(\q=0.19\) and \(0.96\) for marginal multiomics and marginal pseudobulk, respectively). This implies that rs2549785 may contribute to CD risk by altering the accessibility of this peak and, consequently, the expression of \textit{ERAP1} in Bn cells.

\noindent In the second example, the MR methods linked the peak at chr12:56,041,553--56,042,054 (hg38), which contains the T1D-associated variant rs705704, to \textit{ERBB3} in CD4 T cells. This variant lies within the well-established T1D-associated locus at 12q13 \cite{todd2007t1d,hakonarson2008t1d}. Previous work has connected genetic variation at this locus to \textit{ERBB3} expression in peripheral blood mononuclear cells from patients with T1D and controls, with protective genotypes associated with higher expression, and has shown that ERBB3 modulates antigen-presenting cell function in dendritic cells and monocytes \cite{wang2010erbb3}. Functional annotations further indicate that rs705704 lies in a regulatory region: its FAVOR aPC-TF and aPC-EpiActive scores are 29.1 and 18.1, placing it approximately in the top 0.1\% and 1.5\% of variants genome-wide, respectively \cite{zhou2023favor}, and RegulomeDB annotates it as overlapping a transcription factor binding site or an accessible chromatin region \cite{boyle2012regulomedb,dong2023regulomedb}. In our analysis, higher accessibility of this peak was estimated to increase \textit{ERBB3} expression in CD4 T cells (causal IVW \(\hat{\beta}=0.24\), \(\q=1.4\times10^{-4}\)), whereas neither marginal method detected the link (\(\q=0.44\) and \(0.12\), respectively). By linking accessibility of the variant-containing peak to \textit{ERBB3} expression in CD4 T cells, our MR analysis adds a potential cellular context to this T1D-associated locus, suggesting a candidate regulatory mechanism.

\noindent In the third example, the MR methods linked the peak at chr1:155,227,359--155,227,860 (hg38), which contains the IBD-associated variant rs2990245, to \textit{THBS3} in CD8 T cells. Previous integration of blood eQTL and IBD GWAS data prioritized \textit{THBS3} as a candidate IBD target gene through SMR and colocalization (posterior probability $0.96$), with the colocalization replicated in FinnGen \cite{gu2025ibd}, and its elevated expression in gene expression datasets from patients with IBD provides additional disease-related context \cite{zhang2025thbs3}. Regulatory evidence supports a connection between the variant-containing region and \textit{THBS3}: rs2990245 lies 18.6 kb from the \textit{THBS3} TSS and is associated with its expression in GTEx whole blood ($P=5.2\times10^{-33}$) \cite{gtex2017genetic}, and naive CD8 T-cell H3K27ac HiChIP data identified a contact between the 5-kb bin containing the peak and the \textit{THBS3} promoter ($Q=1.9\times10^{-48}$) \cite{chandra2021pieqtl}. FAVOR and RegulomeDB also support the regulatory potential of rs2990245, which has an aPC-TF score of 21.4 (top 0.7\%) \cite{zhou2023favor,boyle2012regulomedb}. In our analysis, higher accessibility of this peak was estimated to decrease \textit{THBS3} expression in CD8 T cells (causal IVW \(\hat{\beta}=-0.30\), \(\q=2.8\times10^{-4}\)), whereas neither marginal method detected the link (\(\q=0.067\) and \(0.45\), respectively). Together, these findings suggest a potential mechanism connecting the IBD-associated variant to altered \textit{THBS3} regulation in CD8 T cells.
