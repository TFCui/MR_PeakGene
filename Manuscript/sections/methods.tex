\noindent CIMA provides hierarchical cell-type annotations. We focused on three major naive lymphocyte populations: Bn\_TCL1A (naive B cells; marker gene \textit{TCL1A}), CD4\_Tn\_CCR7 (naive CD4$^+$ T cells; marker gene \textit{CCR7}), and CD8\_Tn\_CCR7 (naive CD8$^+$ T cells; marker gene \textit{CCR7}), hereafter referred to as Bn cells, CD4 T cells, and CD8 T cells.

\subsection{Study design and terminology}
\label{sec:terminology}
For each candidate peak--gene pair, we used caQTL variants as instruments for chromatin accessibility at the peak (the exposure) and eQTL summary statistics for expression of the gene (the outcome), and estimated the effect of accessibility on expression by Mendelian randomization (MR) within each cell type. We compared these MR estimates with two correlation-based measures of association between the same peak and gene. The MR estimators we used are established methods \cite{burgess2016combining,bowden2015egger,burgess2017interpreting,zhu2016smr,liu2020cauchy}; we retain their original names and describe our implementation in the following subsections.

\noindent We refer to the four MR-based tests (causal IVW, causal Egger, causal SMR, and causal joint) collectively as the causal methods, and to the two correlation-based methods as the marginal methods. Causal IVW and causal Egger are the generalized least squares (GLS) versions of the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) and MR-Egger estimators for correlated instruments, causal SMR is summary-data-based MR, and causal joint combines the $P$ values of causal IVW, causal Egger, and causal SMR by the Cauchy combination test. The marginal methods are Spearman correlations between peak accessibility and gene expression, computed across WNN metacells of an external PBMC dataset (marginal multiomics) or across CIMA individuals within a cell type (marginal pseudobulk). The two labels reflect what each group estimates: MR estimates the causal effect of peak accessibility on gene expression under the instrumental-variable assumptions, whereas the marginal methods estimate associations. $P$ values were adjusted by the Benjamini--Hochberg (BH) procedure within each gene, separately for each method; we denote this BH-adjusted $P$ value by $\q$ and, unless stated otherwise, call a result significant when $\q<0.1$. Each tested peak--gene combination is a peak--gene pair, and a pair that is significant for a given method is a link of that method. An MR-only link is a pair with $\q<0.1$ for at least one of causal IVW, causal Egger, and causal SMR and with $\q\geq0.1$ for both marginal methods; causal joint is not used in this definition. MR-only links are thus supported by MR but not detected by either correlation-based method, and we used them in the gene ontology and heritability enrichment analyses. Details of each method and of the filtering are given in the following subsections.

\subsection{Mendelian randomization}
\label{sec:mr-overview}

Mendelian randomization (MR) uses genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to estimate the causal effect of an exposure on an outcome. A SNP is a valid instrument if it satisfies three assumptions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Relevance:} the SNP is associated with the exposure.
    \item \textbf{Independence:} the SNP is independent of any confounder of the exposure--outcome relationship.
    \item \textbf{Exclusion restriction:} the SNP affects the outcome only through the exposure.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent MR can be performed with summary statistics. For SNP \(j\) (\(j=1,\ldots,J\)), these comprise the estimated SNP--exposure association \(\hat{\gamma}_j\) with standard error \(s_{Xj}\) and the estimated SNP--outcome association \(\hat{\Gamma}_j\) with standard error \(s_{Yj}\), both aligned to the same effect allele. Under a linear model with a common causal effect \(\beta\), the population-level associations satisfy
\begin{equation}
    \Gamma_j = \beta\gamma_j + \alpha_j,
\end{equation}
where \(\beta\gamma_j\) is the component mediated by the exposure and \(\alpha_j\) is the component not mediated by it. A SNP with \(\alpha_j\neq0\) is horizontally pleiotropic and violates the exclusion restriction. For a valid instrument, \(\alpha_j=0\), and the ratio \(\Gamma_j/\gamma_j\) identifies \(\beta\).

\noindent With multiple instruments, MR-Egger regresses the SNP--outcome associations on the SNP--exposure associations with an intercept:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\Gamma}_j = \alpha_0 + \beta\hat{\gamma}_j + \varepsilon_j,
    \qquad
    \varepsilon_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\phi s_{Yj}^{2}),
\end{equation}
where the slope \(\beta\) is the causal effect and \(\phi\) is a residual variance multiplier. Under the Instrument Strength Independent of Direct Effect (InSIDE) assumption, which requires the pleiotropic effects \(\alpha_j\) to be uncorrelated with the SNP--exposure associations \(\gamma_j\), the intercept \(\alpha_0\) can be interpreted as the average pleiotropic effect of the instruments. Constraining the intercept to zero (\(\alpha_0=0\)) gives the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) estimator, a weighted regression through the origin. IVW gives consistent estimates when all instruments are valid, and also when the average pleiotropic effect is zero (balanced pleiotropy) and InSIDE holds; MR-Egger additionally allows for directional pleiotropy under InSIDE \cite{bowden2015egger,burgess2017interpreting}. The IVW and MR-Egger estimators for uncorrelated variants and the conditions under which they are consistent are given in \nameref{S1_Text}.

\noindent In this study, the exposure was chromatin accessibility at an ATAC-seq peak and the outcome was expression of the paired gene. For each candidate peak--gene pair in each cell type, \(\hat{\gamma}_j\) and \(s_{Xj}\) were taken from the CIMA caQTL summary statistics, and \(\hat{\Gamma}_j\) and \(s_{Yj}\) from the CIMA eQTL summary statistics (\nameref{sec:data-preprocessing}). Because the instruments of a pair lie in the same \textit{cis} region and are correlated through LD, we used the GLS versions of IVW and MR-Egger described below.


\subsection{Causal IVW: GLS-IVW with correlated instruments}
\label{sec:gls-ivw}

SNPs within \textit{cis}-regulatory regions are often correlated because of linkage disequilibrium (LD). Clumping stringently enough to obtain independent instruments can leave too few variants per peak--gene pair and thus limit statistical power. Causal IVW therefore uses the GLS extension of IVW, which accounts for the LD that remains among instruments after clumping~\cite{burgess2016combining,burgess2017interpreting}.

\noindent For a given peak--gene pair with \(J\) instruments, let \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=(\hat{\gamma}_1,\ldots,\hat{\gamma}_J)^{\mathsf T}\) and \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}=(\hat{\Gamma}_1,\ldots,\hat{\Gamma}_J)^{\mathsf T}\) be the vectors of estimated SNP--exposure and SNP--outcome associations, let \(\boldsymbol R\) be the signed genotype correlation matrix estimated from the 1000 Genomes East Asian reference panel (\nameref{sec:data-preprocessing}), and let \(\boldsymbol D_Y=\operatorname{diag}(s_{Y1},\ldots,s_{YJ})\) contain the standard errors of the SNP--outcome associations. We approximated the sampling covariance of \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\) by \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\boldsymbol D_Y\boldsymbol R\boldsymbol D_Y\). The IVW model then becomes
\begin{equation}
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}
=
\beta\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
\sim\mathcal{N}_J
(\boldsymbol 0,\phi\boldsymbol{\Sigma}),
\end{equation}
where $\phi$ is the residual variance multiplier of a multiplicative random-effects model. The GLS estimate of the causal effect is
\begin{equation}
\hat{\beta}_{\text{GLS-IVW}}
=
\frac{
\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\mathsf T}
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}
}{
\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\mathsf T}
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
}.
\end{equation}

\noindent
Under strong LD, $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ can be nearly singular, and inverting it directly is numerically unstable. We therefore truncated the eigendecomposition $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\boldsymbol V\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol V^{\mathsf T}$, with eigenvalues $\lambda_1\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_J$ and orthonormal eigenvectors $\boldsymbol v_1,\ldots,\boldsymbol v_J$. We retained the set $\mathcal K$ of leading eigen-directions that together explain 99.9\% of the variance, with a minimum of five directions, and kept only directions whose eigenvalues exceed $10^{-10}$ times the largest eigenvalue; $k=|\mathcal K|$ (formal definition in \nameref{S1_Text}). The truncated matrix $\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}=\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal K}\lambda_\ell\boldsymbol v_\ell\boldsymbol v_\ell^{\mathsf T}$ keeps the $k$ largest eigenvalues of $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$; by the Eckart--Young theorem, it is the best rank-$k$ approximation of $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ in both the Frobenius and spectral norms [CITE: Eckart--Young theorem]. Because $\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}$ is singular when $k<J$, we replaced $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}$ by its Moore--Penrose pseudoinverse $\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{+}=\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal K}\lambda_\ell^{-1}\boldsymbol v_\ell\boldsymbol v_\ell^{\mathsf T}$, which inverts $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ on the retained eigenspace and gives zero weight to the discarded directions. The causal IVW estimate is therefore
\begin{equation}
\hat{\beta}_{\text{GLS-IVW}}
=
\frac{
\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\mathsf T}
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{+}
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}
}{
\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\mathsf T}
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{+}
\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
},
\end{equation}
and, provided that the denominator is positive, its conditional variance under the working covariance model is
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Var}
\left(
\hat{\beta}_{\text{GLS-IVW}}
\mid\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
\right)
=
\frac{\phi}{
\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\mathsf T}
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{+}
\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
}.
\end{equation}

\noindent
The causal effect is the slope $\beta$, and we tested $H_0:\beta=0$. The residual dispersion was estimated as $\hat{\phi}=Q/(J-1)$, where $Q=(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}-\hat{\beta}_{\text{GLS-IVW}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})^{\mathsf T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{+}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}-\hat{\beta}_{\text{GLS-IVW}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})$ is the residual quadratic form and $J$ is the number of instruments after LD filtering, not the number of retained eigen-directions $k$. Pleiotropic instruments make the SNP-specific ratio estimates more heterogeneous than sampling error alone would, so the residual variance can exceed that implied by $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ (overdispersion). We accommodated this with the multiplicative random-effects model by setting $\widetilde{\phi}=\max(1,\hat{\phi})$, which inflates the standard error under overdispersion but never makes it smaller than under the fixed-effect model ($\phi=1$) (details in \nameref{S1_Text}). The standard error was $\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat{\beta}_{\text{GLS-IVW}})=\{\widetilde{\phi}/(\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\mathsf T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{+}\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\}^{1/2}$, and the two-sided $P$ value was obtained by comparing $T=\hat{\beta}_{\text{GLS-IVW}}/\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat{\beta}_{\text{GLS-IVW}})$ with a $t$ distribution with $J-1$ degrees of freedom.


\subsection{Causal Egger: GLS MR-Egger with correlated instruments}
\label{sec:gls-egger}

Causal Egger extends the causal IVW model by adding an intercept \cite{burgess2017interpreting}. With the association vectors and LD-based covariance matrix defined above, the model is
\begin{equation}
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}
=
\alpha_0\boldsymbol 1_J
+\beta\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{Egger}},
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{Egger}}
\sim
\mathcal N_J
(\boldsymbol 0,\phi_{\mathrm{Egger}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}),
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol 1_J$ is a vector of ones, $\alpha_0$ is the intercept, and $\phi_{\mathrm{Egger}}$ is the residual variance multiplier. As in causal IVW, the estimated SNP--exposure associations $\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}$ were treated as fixed under the NO Measurement Error (NOME) assumption (\nameref{S1_Text}), and the pseudoinverse $\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{+}$ defined above was used as the weighting matrix. Define
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol X
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\boldsymbol 1_J & \hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\theta}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\alpha_0\\
\beta
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Provided that
$\boldsymbol X^{\mathsf T}
 \widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{+}\boldsymbol X$
is nonsingular, the GLS estimates are
\begin{equation}
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{GLS-Egger}}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\hat{\alpha}_0\\
\hat{\beta}_{\text{GLS-Egger}}
\end{pmatrix}
=
\left(
\boldsymbol X^{\mathsf T}
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{+}\boldsymbol X
\right)^{-1}
\boldsymbol X^{\mathsf T}
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{+}
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}},
\end{equation}
with conditional covariance matrix
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Var}
\left(
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{GLS-Egger}}
\mid\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
\right)
=
\phi_{\mathrm{Egger}}
\left(
\boldsymbol X^{\mathsf T}
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{+}\boldsymbol X
\right)^{-1}
\end{equation}
under the working covariance model. Inference on the slope followed causal IVW. The residual dispersion $\hat{\phi}_{\mathrm{Egger}}$ was the residual quadratic form $Q_{\mathrm{Egger}}=(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}-\boldsymbol X\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{GLS-Egger}})^{\mathsf T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{+}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}-\boldsymbol X\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{GLS-Egger}})$ divided by $J-2$; the slope standard error was the square root of the second diagonal element of the covariance matrix, with $\phi_{\mathrm{Egger}}$ replaced by $\widetilde{\phi}_{\mathrm{Egger}}=\max(1,\hat{\phi}_{\mathrm{Egger}})$; and two-sided $P$ values for $H_0:\beta=0$ were obtained from a $t$ distribution with $J-2$ degrees of freedom. Causal Egger estimates depend on the allele orientation of the instruments, whereas causal IVW estimates do not (\nameref{sec:orientation}).


\subsection{Causal SMR}
\label{sec:smr}
Summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR) estimates the relationship
between an exposure and an outcome from the summary-level associations of
a single genetic instrument with each of them \citep{zhu2016smr}.
We applied this approach to chromatin accessibility and gene expression.
For each peak--gene pair, we selected the SNP with the smallest nominal
caQTL $P$ value among the variants retained after the filtering described
above, breaking ties by the largest absolute ratio of the caQTL effect to its
standard error. Denoting this SNP by \(*\), the effect estimate is
\begin{equation}
\hat\beta_{\mathrm{SMR}}=\frac{\hat\Gamma_*}{\hat\gamma_*}.
\end{equation}
The SMR test uses a delta-method approximation to the variance of this
ratio that accounts for uncertainty in both associations
\citep{zhu2016smr}. In our implementation, this approximation omitted the
sampling covariance between the caQTL and eQTL effect estimates.

For the selected SNP, we converted the two-sided nominal caQTL and eQTL
$P$ values, \(p_X\) and \(p_Y\), into nonnegative normal-equivalent statistics:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
z_X&=\Phi^{-1}(1-\tilde p_X/2),&
z_Y&=\Phi^{-1}(1-\tilde p_Y/2),\\
\tilde p_X&=\max(p_X,10^{-300}),&
\tilde p_Y&=\max(p_Y,10^{-300}),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal cumulative distribution function;
numerically, the inverse was evaluated on the upper tail.
From these $P$-derived statistics, we calculated
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat\beta_{\mathrm{SMR}})
&=|\hat\beta_{\mathrm{SMR}}|\sqrt{z_X^{-2}+z_Y^{-2}},\\
T_{\mathrm{SMR}}&=\frac{z_X^2z_Y^2}{z_X^2+z_Y^2}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The SMR $P$ value was the upper-tail probability of \(T_{\mathrm{SMR}}\)
under an approximate \(\chi_1^2\) reference distribution. The sign of the effect
was given by the ratio of the aligned association estimates before the allele orientation step (\nameref{sec:orientation}). We did not perform the heterogeneity in dependent instruments (HEIDI) test; SMR significance
alone does not distinguish mediation from horizontal pleiotropy or from linkage
between distinct causal variants \citep{zhu2016smr}.


\subsection{Causal joint: Cauchy combination}
\label{sec:cct}
The Cauchy combination test (CCT) is an efficient and powerful technique to combine multiple individual $P$ values, especially when the tests are correlated \cite{liu2020cauchy}. Under the null hypothesis, for an individual $P$ value $p_k$, the transformed statistic follows a standard Cauchy distribution. We calculated the causal joint results by aggregating the three causal methods (causal IVW, causal Egger, and causal SMR) using the Cauchy combination test with equal weights:
\begin{equation*}
T = \frac{1}{3}\left\{\tan\left[\pi(0.5 - p_{\mathrm{IVW}})\right] + \tan\left[\pi(0.5 - p_{\mathrm{Egger}})\right] + \tan\left[\pi(0.5 - p_{\mathrm{SMR}})\right]\right\},
\end{equation*}
where $p_{\mathrm{IVW}}$, $p_{\mathrm{Egger}}$, and $p_{\mathrm{SMR}}$ denote the nominal $P$ values from GLS-IVW, GLS MR-Egger, and SMR, respectively. The combined $P$ value is
\begin{equation*}
p_{\mathrm{joint}} = \frac{1}{2}-\frac{\arctan T}{\pi},
\end{equation*}
computed as $\arctan(1/T)/\pi$ when $T>0$ for numerical accuracy. For $p<10^{-15}$, the term $\tan[\pi(0.5-p)]$ was replaced by its tail approximation $1/(\pi p)$, and for $p>1-10^{-15}$ by $-1/\{\pi(1-p)\}$. The causal joint results can be understood as the aggregated causal evidence for the studied peak--gene links.


\subsection{Marginal pseudobulk correlation}
\label{sec:pseudobulk-correlations}

We evaluated marginal peak--gene associations using pseudobulk RNA and
ATAC profiles within each cell type. The starting point was the CIMA count matrices for all cells (RNA: 6,484,974 cells and 36,326 genes; ATAC: 3,762,242 cells and 338,036 peaks), which were subset to each cell type using the CIMA level-4 annotation. For each modality, individuals were retained if they had at least 100 cells and no more than the median plus three median absolute deviations (MAD scaled by 1.4826) of the per-individual cell number; individuals passing in both modalities were used, yielding 335 individuals for Bn cells, 372 for CD4 T cells, and 346 for CD8 T cells. Counts were summed across
cells from the same individual within each cell type to construct
gene-by-individual and peak-by-individual pseudobulk matrices (scanpy 1.10.4).
For each modality, counts were normalized using the individual-level library size $L$, the sum of counts across all features of that modality, and transformed as $A=\ln(1+10^{6}C/L)$, where $C$ is the summed count.

\noindent For each candidate peak--gene pair, we calculated Spearman's
rank correlation between peak accessibility and gene expression across
matched individuals:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\rho}^{\mathrm{PB}}_{pg}
=
\operatorname{Cor}_{\mathrm{S}}\left(
\boldsymbol{A}_{p}, \boldsymbol{E}_{g}
\right),
\end{equation}
where \(\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\) and \(\boldsymbol{E}_{g}\) are the
log-transformed CPM vectors for peak \(p\) and gene \(g\), respectively,
across matched individuals, and \(\operatorname{Cor}_{\mathrm{S}}\)
denotes Spearman's rank correlation, with tied values assigned average ranks.
Two-sided approximate $P$ values were obtained using R's
\texttt{cor.test} with \texttt{method="spearman"} and
\texttt{exact=FALSE}, which uses a $t$ approximation with $n-2$ degrees of freedom. No covariate residualization was applied at the
correlation stage. All pairs in a cell type used the same individuals, with no missing values. $P$ values were adjusted as described in \nameref{sec:filtering}, and peak--gene pairs
with \(\q<0.1\) were considered significant.

\subsection{Marginal multiomics: WNN metacell correlation}
\label{sec:wnn-correlations}

We evaluated marginal peak--gene associations using an external PBMC
multiome dataset with 80 precomputed weighted nearest-neighbor (WNN)
metacell groups. We used the existing WNN metacell groups supplied with the dataset and did not recluster the cells. The same RNA and ATAC metacell profiles were used
for all three CIMA cell-type analyses, without restricting metacells
to the corresponding cell type. WNN defined the cell groups, whereas
peak--gene associations were quantified by correlations across
metacells.

To align accessibility features with CIMA, external ATAC fragments
were recounted over all 338,036 CIMA ATAC peak intervals in hg38 using Signac's
\texttt{FeatureMatrix}, after excluding intervals that overlapped
other peaks in the input set. ATAC counts were summed within each

transformation was then applied to both the normalized ATAC matrix
and the supplied RNA matrix.

For each candidate peak--gene pair, we calculated Spearman's rank
correlation between peak accessibility and gene expression across
the 80 metacells:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\rho}^{\mathrm{WNN}}_{pg}
=
\operatorname{Cor}_{\mathrm{S}}\left(
\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathrm{MC}}_{p},
\boldsymbol{E}^{\mathrm{MC}}_{g}
\right),
\end{equation}
where \(\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathrm{MC}}_{p}\) and
\(\boldsymbol{E}^{\mathrm{MC}}_{g}\) denote the transformed
accessibility and expression vectors for peak \(p\) and gene \(g\),
respectively, across metacells, and
\(\operatorname{Cor}_{\mathrm{S}}\) denotes Spearman's rank
correlation. Metacells served as the units of observation.
Two-sided approximate $P$ values were calculated as described in
\nameref{sec:pseudobulk-correlations}. No covariate residualization,
cell-type adjustment, or weighting by metacell size was applied.
The resulting correlations therefore include variation across PBMC
cell types and states. Because one matrix was used for all three cell types, a given pair has the same $\widehat{\rho}^{\mathrm{WNN}}$ and nominal $P$ value in each cell type, but its $\q$ can differ because the set of pairs tested for each gene differs between cell types. $P$ values were adjusted as described in \nameref{sec:filtering}, separately for each
CIMA cell-type analysis, and peak--gene pairs with
\(\q<0.1\) were considered significant.


\subsection{Filtering and multiple testing}
\label{sec:filtering}
After all methods had been applied, we first removed pairs for which any of the five base methods (causal IVW, causal Egger, causal SMR, marginal multiomics and marginal pseudobulk) returned a missing $P$ value. We then removed pairs with $\hat\phi_{\mathrm{IVW}}>10$: at this level of residual dispersion the GLS fit had failed, typically because of outlying instruments or a near-singular LD matrix among highly correlated variants. Causal joint $P$ values were computed for the remaining pairs. Finally, each of the six sets of $P$ values was adjusted by the Benjamini--Hochberg procedure within each gene, separately for each method and cell type \citep{benjamini1995fdr}, giving $\q$ (\nameref{sec:terminology}). Discovery used \(\q<0.1\).

\subsection{Hypergeometric enrichment}
\label{sec:hyper}

We tested whether the significant peaks of each method were enriched for peaks that overlap existing enhancer and chromatin interaction references: FANTOM5 [CITE: FANTOM5 enhancer atlas] linked enhancers and genes based on enhancer RNA expression; 4DGenome [CITE: 4DGenome] linked enhancers and genes based on physical interactions using chromatin-looping data; EnhancerAtlas [CITE: EnhancerAtlas] reported enhancers using 12 high-throughput experimental methods; cis-eQTLs in whole blood mapped in European-American subjects were obtained from the GTEx Consortium [CITE: GTEx]; promoter capture Hi-C (PCHi-C) captured contacts between gene promoters and distal regions [CITE: PCHi-C in immune cells]; and H3K27ac HiChIP captured contacts among regions marked by an active regulatory histone modification [CITE: H3K27ac HiChIP; Supplementary Table 3 of Nat Genet article 41588\_2020\_745]. For HiChIP, we used interacting regions with $Q<0.1$ in the B cell, CD4 T cell and CD8 T cell columns; for PCHi-C, we used the other ends of contacts with CHiCAGO score $\geq 5$ in naive B cells, naive CD4 T cells and naive CD8 T cells. For GTEx, we used variants with meta-analysis $P<0.05$ and removed variants located within transcripts annotated in EnsDb.Hsapiens.v86 (2.99.0). Only the HiChIP and PCHi-C regions were lifted over from hg19 to hg38, using rtracklayer (1.66.0) with the \texttt{hg19ToHg38.over.chain} file; regions that failed to map or mapped to more than one chromosome were discarded, and regions that mapped to multiple segments were represented by the range from the first to the last segment.

\noindent For each cell type and method, and within each gene, let $N$ be the number of candidate peaks, $K$ the number of these peaks that overlap the gene's reference regions, $n_{\mathrm{sig}}$ the number of peaks significant for the method ($\q<0.1$), and $k$ the number of peaks that are both significant and overlapping. \subsection{GRN overlap}
\label{sec:grn}
We compared the links of each method with the CIMA SCENIC+ (1.0.1) enhancer-driven regulons (eRegulons) \cite{yin2026cima} [CITE: SCENIC+]. We used two sets: the eRegulons active in the cell type (cell-type eRegulons) and the subset of these that belong to the high-quality eRegulons of the corresponding lineage (high-quality eRegulons). From each set we extracted the unique region--gene pairs. For each cell type and method, we built a $2\times2$ table of all tested pairs in the cell type, cross-classified by whether the pair was significant ($\q<0.05$) and whether it was present in the eRegulon set, and applied Fisher's exact test. We report the odds ratio (Fig~\ref{fig:go}E; \nameref{S8_Fig}).


\subsection{Heritability enrichment analysis from S-LDSC}
\label{sec:sldsc}
We used stratified LD score regression (S-LDSC; LDSC v1.0.1) \cite{finucane2015sldsc} to test whether peaks of MR-only links were enriched for disease heritability. The MR-only peaks were the union of the peaks of MR-only links (\nameref{sec:terminology}) across the three cell types. The comparison set was all candidate peaks (316,798 peaks in hg19). Peaks were lifted over from hg38 to hg19 with rtracklayer (1.66.0), keeping only one-to-one mappings on chromosomes 1--22, extended by 500 bp on each side and merged. The annotation was the set of SNPs located in the extended peaks. We fitted S-LDSC separately for the MR-only annotation and for the all-candidate-peak annotation, each together with the 97 baselineLD v2.2 annotations, with \texttt{--ld-wind-cm 1}, LD scores restricted to HapMap3 SNPs, and \texttt{--overlap-annot --print-coefficients}. We used the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 European LD reference data and the standard regression weights that exclude the MHC region; the reference files were obtained from Zenodo (doi:10.5281/zenodo.10515792). Enrichment was defined as the proportion of heritability explained by the annotation divided by the proportion of SNPs in the annotation, and we report the enrichment estimate and its $P$ value for the tested annotation.

\noindent We report seven immune-mediated diseases: ankylosing spondylitis (AS), Crohn's disease (CD), inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), rheumatoid arthritis (RA), systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), multiple sclerosis (MS), and type 1 diabetes (T1D). Summary statistics for IBD \cite{delange2017ibd}, RA \cite{saevarsdottir2022ra}, SLE \cite{bentham2015sle} and T1D \cite{chiou2021t1d} were obtained from the collection of 107 independent GWAS summary statistics on Zenodo (\url{https://zenodo.org/records/10515792/files/sumstats_indep107.tgz}). Summary statistics for AS were obtained from the Pan-UK Biobank (\url{https://pan.ukbb.broadinstitute.org/}), those for CD from the International IBD Genetics Consortium (\url{https://www.ibdgenetics.org/}), and those for MS from ImmunoBase \cite{sawcer2011ms}. The sample sizes and sources of the seven GWAS are given in \nameref{S4_Table}.

\subsection{Motif enrichment analysis}
\label{sec:motif}
We performed motif enrichment analysis to determine whether transcription factor binding motifs were overrepresented in significant peaks compared with background peaks. For each cell type (Bn cells, CD4 T cells, CD8 T cells) and each of the six methods, we used the peaks of links with $\q<0.1$ as the foreground set. The universe was the peaks of the candidate pairs that were also present in the ATAC object of that cell type (316,117 peaks in Bn cells), and the background was 50,000 peaks drawn from this universe with \texttt{MatchRegionStats}, matched for GC content and $\log(1+\text{total accessibility})$. Motif occurrences were annotated with \texttt{AddMotifs} in Signac (1.14.0) [CITE: Signac] using the JASPAR2020 CORE vertebrate collection (JASPAR2020 0.99.10; latest version of each matrix, 746 motifs) [CITE: JASPAR2020] and the hg38 genome (BSgenome.Hsapiens.UCSC.hg38 1.4.5). We used \texttt{FindMotifs} in Signac, which computes $P$ values with a hypergeometric test, and adjusted the $P$ values by the Benjamini--Hochberg procedure across motifs within each cell type and method. Motifs with adjusted $P<0.05$ and fold enrichment $>1$ were considered enriched. A motif was MR-specific if it was enriched for at least one of causal IVW, causal Egger and causal SMR and for neither marginal method; causal joint was not used in this definition. For Table~\ref{tab:motif}, we selected for each cell type the two MR-specific motifs with the smallest adjusted $P$ among the causal methods, skipping motifs without a clear literature report.


\subsection{Allele orientation}
\label{sec:orientation}
Before fitting causal IVW and causal Egger, we flipped the sign of $\hat{\gamma}_j$ and $\hat{\Gamma}_j$, together with the corresponding row and column of $\boldsymbol R$, for every SNP with $\hat{\gamma}_j<0$, so that all SNP--exposure associations were positive. This orientation does not change causal IVW estimates but does change causal Egger estimates. We compared causal Egger with and without this orientation across 176,545 pairs (\nameref{S2_Fig}).


\subsection{Sensitivity analysis: Overlapping samples}
\label{sec:overlap}
Consider the two marginal models of the caQTL and eQTL studies, which provide the exposure and outcome associations in the MR framework. For each SNP separately, the marginal associations can be represented as
\begin{align*}
X_i &= \mu_{Xj} + \gamma_j G_{ij} + \varepsilon_{ij}^{X}, \\
Y_i &= \mu_{Yj} + \Gamma_j G_{ij} + \varepsilon_{ij}^{Y},
\end{align*}
where \(\mu_{Xj}\) and \(\mu_{Yj}\) are intercepts, and \(\gamma_j\) and \(\Gamma_j\) are the population-level SNP--exposure and SNP--outcome associations, respectively. MR study is usually base on Two-Sample data, where the SNP-exposure estimates and SNP-outcome estimates come from two independent but homogeneous samples. In this ways, the residuals $\varepsilon_{ij}^{X}$ and $\varepsilon_{ij}^{Y}$ are independent. However, in our study, caQTL and eQTL come from the same population, leading to some potential bias. In order to measures of the bias, we do the following sensitivity test for each of cell types separately.

\noindent We first performed LD pruning (\texttt{--indep-pairwise 500kb 50 0.1}, MAF $\geq 0.01$, with MAF computed in the reference panel) on the caQTL variants on chromosomes 1--22 that matched the EAS reference panel. We further selected SNPs under the null (caQTL nominal $P>0.05$ and eQTL nominal $P>0.05$). The marginal associations are under the null, there is no evidence to prove the marginal associations ($\gamma_j$ and $\Gamma_j$) are nonzero values. Therefore, we can use the correlation of Z scores to approximate the correlation of residuals:

\begin{equation*}
z_{Xj} = \frac{\hat{\gamma}_j}{s_{Xj}}, \qquad z_{Yj} = \frac{\hat{\Gamma}_j}{s_{Yj}}.
\end{equation*}


\noindent We calculated the genome-wide correlation of $z_{Xj}$ and $z_{Yj}$ for each cell type by pooling all (pair, SNP) observations: 17,074,938 (Bn cells), 33,263,695 (CD4 T cells) and 21,537,822 (CD8 T cells) observations. We also calculated the correlation of $z_{Xj}$ and $z_{Yj}$ for each peak--gene pair with more than two SNPs after LD pruning (547,452, 1,051,953 and 686,829 pairs, respectively), tested with a $t$ test with $n-2$ degrees of freedom. In this analysis, alleles were aligned by flipping the sign of the eQTL slope for variants with reversed alleles, rather than by removing them as in the main MR analysis. The distributions of correlations and $P$ values are shown in \nameref{S1_Fig}.
