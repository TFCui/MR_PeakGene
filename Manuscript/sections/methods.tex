\section{Materials and Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Data sources}

The Chinese Immune Multi-Omics Atlas (CIMA, accession OMIX009786) profiled peripheral blood immune cells from 428 Chinese individuals \cite{yin2026cima}. For our Mendelian randomization analyses, we used CIMA's cell-type-specific summary statistics for expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) and chromatin accessibility quantitative trait loci (caQTLs). To map cis-eQTLs, CIMA tested SNPs within 1~Mb of each gene's transcription start site (TSS) using TensorQTL (version 1.0.10) \cite{taylorweiner2019tensorqtl}. To map cis-caQTLs, CIMA tested SNPs within 1~Mb of each accessibility peak's midpoint \cite{yin2026cima}. Within each cell type, we considered genes with available CIMA eQTL summary statistics that were also present in the pseudobulk RNA matrix. For each such gene, we defined candidate peak--gene pairs by including every peak in the CIMA ATAC data whose midpoint lay within 1~Mb of the gene's TSS.

\noindent We also used the CIMA single-cell RNA and ATAC count matrices to construct donor-level pseudobulk profiles. Based on the CIMA level-4 annotations, we focused on three naive lymphocyte populations: naive B cells (Bn\_TCL1A), naive CD4$^+$ T cells (CD4\_Tn\_CCR7), and naive CD8$^+$ T cells (CD8\_Tn\_CCR7). We refer to these populations as Bn cells, CD4 T cells, and CD8 T cells, respectively.

\noindent For the metacell correlation analysis, we used the publicly available 10k peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) dataset from 10x Genomics \cite{tenx2020pbmc10k}. PBMCs were obtained from a healthy 25-year-old female donor, and granulocytes were removed by cell sorting before nuclei isolation. The Chromium Single Cell Multiome ATAC + Gene Expression platform provided paired measurements of chromatin accessibility and gene expression from the same nuclei. We used the metacell assignments and RNA metacell profiles provided by the TRIPOD study \cite{jiang2022tripod} and quantified accessibility from the 10x ATAC fragments over CIMA peak intervals.

\subsection{Causal IVW}
\label{sec:gls-ivw}

We used Mendelian randomization (MR) to estimate the causal effect of chromatin accessibility at a candidate peak (the exposure) on expression of the paired gene (the outcome), with single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as instrumental variables. A valid instrument is associated with the exposure, is independent of any confounder of the exposure--outcome relationship, and affects the outcome only through the exposure~\cite{lawlor2008mendelian}; under these assumptions, MR avoids the confounding that affects marginal correlations (\nameref{S1_Text}, Sections~1 and 2). For each candidate peak--gene pair with $J$ instruments, let \(\hat{\gamma}_j\) and \(s_{Xj}\) denote the estimated SNP--exposure association of SNP $j$ and its standard error from the CIMA caQTL summary statistics, and let \(\hat{\Gamma}_j\) and \(s_{Yj}\) denote the corresponding SNP--outcome association and its standard error from the CIMA eQTL summary statistics (\nameref{sec:data-preprocessing}). Under the NO Measurement Error (NOME) assumption \cite{bowden2016nome}, we treated the estimated SNP--exposure associations as fixed, ignoring their sampling error. When NOME is violated, this measurement error attenuates causal estimates toward zero, and, because our caQTL and eQTL data come from overlapping samples, correlated estimation errors bias the estimates toward the marginal association; both biases are approximately inversely proportional to instrument strength (\nameref{S1_Text}, Sections~3 and 4; \nameref{sec:overlap}). \nameref{S1_Text} (Sections~5 and 6) describes the standard IVW and MR-Egger estimators for uncorrelated SNPs; causal IVW and causal Egger extend them to correlated \textit{cis} SNPs.

\noindent SNPs within \textit{cis}-regulatory regions are often correlated because of linkage disequilibrium (LD). Stringent LD clumping to obtain independent instruments can leave too few variants per peak--gene pair, potentially limiting statistical power. We therefore used the generalized least squares (GLS) extension of IVW to account for correlations among instruments~\cite{burgess2016combining,burgess2017interpreting}.

\noindent For a given peak--gene pair with $J$ instruments, let $\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=(\hat{\gamma}_1,\ldots,\hat{\gamma}_J)^{\mathsf T}$ and $\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}=(\hat{\Gamma}_1,\ldots,\hat{\Gamma}_J)^{\mathsf T}$ denote the vectors of estimated SNP--exposure and SNP--outcome associations, respectively. Let $\boldsymbol R$ denote the signed genotype correlation matrix estimated from the reference panel (\nameref{sec:data-preprocessing}), and let $\boldsymbol D_Y=\operatorname{diag}(s_{Y1},\ldots,s_{YJ})$ contain the standard errors of the SNP--outcome associations. We approximated the sampling covariance matrix of $\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}$ by $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\boldsymbol D_Y\boldsymbol R\boldsymbol D_Y$. Consistent with NOME, $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ describes only the sampling errors of $\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}$; it does not model the sampling errors of $\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}$ or their covariance with those of $\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}$ induced by sample overlap (\nameref{S1_Text}, Sections~4 and 7). The causal IVW model is
\begin{equation*}
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}
=
\beta\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
\sim\mathcal{N}_J
(\boldsymbol 0,\phi\boldsymbol{\Sigma}),
\end{equation*}
where \(\beta\) is the causal effect and \(\phi\) is a residual variance multiplier, with \(\phi=1\) corresponding to the fixed-effect model (\nameref{S1_Text}, Section~8). Causal IVW provides consistent estimates when all instruments are valid or, more generally, when pleiotropy is balanced and unrelated to instrument strength (\nameref{S1_Text}, Sections~3 and 5). Using standard results from generalized least squares regression, the causal effect estimator has the closed-form expression
\begin{equation*}
\hat{\beta}_{\text{IVW}}
=
\frac{
\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\mathsf T}
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}
}{
\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\mathsf T}
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
}.
\end{equation*}

\noindent Under strong LD, $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ may be singular, making direct inversion numerically unstable. We therefore used a truncated eigendecomposition of $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\boldsymbol V\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol V^{\mathsf T}$, with eigenvalues $\lambda_1\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_J$ and orthonormal eigenvectors $\boldsymbol v_1,\ldots,\boldsymbol v_J$. We retained the leading eigenvalues that together accounted for 99.9\% of the total positive eigenvalue sum (\nameref{S1_Text}, Section~9). Let $\mathcal K$ denote the indices of the $k$ retained leading eigenvalues. The truncated matrix $\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}=\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal K}\lambda_\ell\boldsymbol v_\ell\boldsymbol v_\ell^{\mathsf T}$ retains the $k$ largest eigenvalues of $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ (\nameref{S1_Text}, Section~9). By the Eckart--Young theorem, it is the best rank-$k$ approximation to $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ in both the Frobenius and spectral norms \cite{eckart1936approximation}. Its Moore--Penrose pseudoinverse is $\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{+}=\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal K}\lambda_\ell^{-1}\boldsymbol v_\ell\boldsymbol v_\ell^{\mathsf T}$. The causal effect estimate is
\begin{equation*}
\hat{\beta}_{\text{IVW}}
=
\frac{
\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\mathsf T}
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{+}
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}
}{
\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\mathsf T}
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{+}
\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
}.
\end{equation*}

\noindent The residual dispersion was estimated as $\widetilde{\phi}=\frac{(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}-\hat{\beta}_{\text{IVW}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})^{\mathsf T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{+}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}-\hat{\beta}_{\text{IVW}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})}{J-1}$. Pleiotropy among the instruments may introduce heterogeneity in the causal effect estimates obtained from individual genetic variants. To accommodate this potential overdispersion, we used a multiplicative random-effects model with $\hat{\phi}=\max(1,\widetilde{\phi})$ (details in \nameref{S1_Text}, Section~8). Because $\phi$ is a common variance multiplier, this adjustment leaves the point estimate unchanged and only inflates the standard error, which is never smaller than under the fixed-effect model. Pairs with $\hat{\phi}>10$ were excluded (\nameref{sec:filtering}). The standard error was calculated as $\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat{\beta}_{\text{IVW}})=\{\hat{\phi}/(\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\mathsf T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{+}\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\}^{1/2}$. Two-sided $P$ values for testing $H_0:\beta=0$ were obtained by comparing $T=\hat{\beta}_{\text{IVW}}/\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat{\beta}_{\text{IVW}})$ with a $t$ distribution with $J-1$ degrees of freedom.

\subsection{Causal Egger}
\label{sec:gls-egger}
Causal Egger extends MR-Egger to correlated SNPs by generalized least squares (GLS). With the association vectors and LD-based covariance matrix defined above, the model is
\begin{equation*}
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}
=
\alpha_0\boldsymbol 1_J
+\beta\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
\sim
\mathcal N_J
(\boldsymbol 0,\phi \boldsymbol{\Sigma}),
\end{equation*}
where $\boldsymbol 1_J$ is a vector of ones and $\phi$ is the residual variance multiplier. The intercept $\alpha_0$ represents the weighted average horizontal pleiotropic effect, that is, the average effect of the instruments on the outcome that is not mediated by the exposure (\nameref{S1_Text}, Section~2). Under the Instrument Strength Independent of Direct Effect (InSIDE) assumption, which requires the pleiotropic effects to be unrelated to instrument strength (\nameref{S1_Text}, Section~3), the slope $\beta$ remains consistent even with directional pleiotropy ($\alpha_0\neq0$), and the intercept estimates the weighted average pleiotropic effect \cite{bowden2015egger,burgess2017interpreting}. As in causal IVW, the estimated SNP--exposure associations $\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}$ were treated as fixed under NOME, and the pseudoinverse $\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{+}$ defined above was used as the weighting matrix. Define $\boldsymbol{X}=[\boldsymbol{1}_J\;\;\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}]$ and $\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\alpha_0,\beta)^{\mathsf T}$. The causal Egger estimate is:
\begin{equation*}
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{Egger}}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\hat{\alpha}_0\\
\hat{\beta}_{\text{Egger}}
\end{pmatrix}
=
\left(
\boldsymbol X^{\mathsf T}
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{+}\boldsymbol X
\right)^{-1}
\boldsymbol X^{\mathsf T}
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{+}
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}.
\end{equation*}

\noindent Unlike the IVW estimate, the Egger estimate depends on which allele is coded as the effect allele, so we oriented all instruments to the accessibility-increasing allele before fitting (\nameref{sec:orientation}). With correlated instruments, the InSIDE condition takes a GLS-weighted form (\nameref{S1_Text}, Section~7). Because the Egger slope is estimated from the variation of $\hat{\gamma}_j$ across instruments rather than from their magnitude, causal Egger is more sensitive than causal IVW to measurement error in $\hat{\gamma}_j$ \cite{bowden2016nome}.

\noindent Inference followed the same procedure as for causal IVW. Let $\boldsymbol{r}_{\mathrm{Egger}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}-\boldsymbol{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{Egger}}$ denote the residual vector. The standard error was $\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}_{\text{Egger}})=\left\{\max\left(1,\frac{\boldsymbol{r}_{\mathrm{Egger}}^{\mathsf T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{+}\boldsymbol{r}_{\mathrm{Egger}}}{J-2}\right)\left[\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{\mathsf T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{+}\boldsymbol{X}\right)^{-1}\right]_{22}\right\}^{1/2}$, where $[\cdot]_{22}$ denotes the second diagonal element. Two-sided $P$ values for $H_0:\beta=0$ were obtained from a $t$ distribution with $J-2$ degrees of freedom.


\subsection{Causal SMR}
\label{sec:smr}

Causal SMR is based on summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR) \cite{zhu2016smr}, which estimates and tests the effect of an exposure on an outcome using a single genetic variant and summary statistics from two studies. For a variant with effect $\gamma$ on the exposure and effect $\Gamma$ on the outcome, MR with a single instrument estimates the effect of the exposure on the outcome by the ratio $\Gamma/\gamma$, which is free of confounding by non-genetic factors \cite{zhu2016smr}. SMR replaces the two effects with their estimates from the exposure and outcome studies and tests the ratio with an approximate $\chi^2_1$ statistic derived from the delta-method variance of the ratio \cite{zhu2016smr}:
\begin{equation*}
\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{SMR}}=\frac{\hat{\Gamma}}{\hat{\gamma}},
\qquad
T_{\mathrm{SMR}}=\frac{z_X^2z_Y^2}{z_X^2+z_Y^2},
\end{equation*}
where $z_X$ and $z_Y$ are the $z$ statistics of the variant in the exposure and outcome studies. Equivalently, $1/T_{\mathrm{SMR}}=1/z_X^2+1/z_Y^2$, so $T_{\mathrm{SMR}}$ is large only when the variant is strongly associated with both traits; a strong association with only one of them does not produce a significant result. The derivation of $T_{\mathrm{SMR}}$ sets the covariance between $\hat{\gamma}$ and $\hat{\Gamma}$ to zero, which holds when the two effects are estimated from independent samples \cite{zhu2016smr}; in our setting, this covariance is determined by the error correlation induced by sample overlap (\nameref{sec:overlap}). Because it uses a single variant, SMR cannot distinguish a causal effect of the exposure from pleiotropy, in which the variant affects the exposure and the outcome through separate pathways \cite{zhu2016smr}. Under the instrumental variable assumptions, including the exclusion restriction, $\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{SMR}}$ can be interpreted as an estimate of the causal effect.

\noindent In our study, for each peak--gene pair we used the lead SNP, the instrument with the smallest nominal caQTL $P$ value, and denote it by $*$. The causal SMR estimate is $\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{SMR}}=\hat{\Gamma}_*/\hat{\gamma}_*$. Let $p_X$ and $p_Y$ denote the two-sided nominal caQTL and eQTL $P$ values of the lead SNP. We converted them to absolute $z$ statistics, $z_k=\Phi^{-1}(1-p_k/2)$ for $k\in\{X,Y\}$, where $\Phi$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function, computed $T_{\mathrm{SMR}}$ as above, and obtained the $P$ value from the $\chi^2_1$ distribution. With a single instrument, the causal IVW estimate reduces to the same Wald ratio; causal SMR therefore uses only the lead caQTL SNP, whereas causal IVW combines the ratio estimates of all instruments with inverse-variance weights and accounts for their LD.

\subsection{Causal joint}
\label{sec:cct}
The Cauchy combination test (CCT) is an efficient and powerful technique to combine multiple individual $P$ values \cite{liu2020cauchy}. We calculated the causal joint results by aggregating the three causal methods (causal IVW, causal Egger, and causal SMR) using the Cauchy combination test with equal weights:
\begin{equation*}
T = \frac{1}{3}\left\{\tan\left[\pi(0.5 - p_{\mathrm{IVW}})\right] + \tan\left[\pi(0.5 - p_{\mathrm{Egger}})\right] + \tan\left[\pi(0.5 - p_{\mathrm{SMR}})\right]\right\},
\end{equation*}
where $p_{\mathrm{IVW}}$, $p_{\mathrm{Egger}}$, and $p_{\mathrm{SMR}}$ denote the nominal $P$ values from causal IVW, causal Egger, and causal SMR, respectively. The combined $P$ value is
\begin{equation*}
p_{\mathrm{joint}} = \frac{1}{2}-\frac{\arctan T}{\pi}.
\end{equation*}
Under the global null hypothesis, in which all three $P$ values are uniformly distributed, $T$ approximately follows a standard Cauchy distribution even when the individual $P$ values are correlated, because the tail of $T$ is insensitive to their dependence \cite{liu2020cauchy}. This property is important here, because the three tests share instruments and their $P$ values are therefore correlated. Because $T$ is dominated by the smallest individual $P$ values, causal joint aggregates the causal evidence for a peak--gene pair and is powerful when at least one of the three tests provides strong evidence.


\subsection{Marginal pseudobulk correlations}
\label{sec:pseudobulk-correlations}

Within each cell type, we assessed whether peak accessibility and gene expression were correlated across individuals. Following the quality control described in \nameref{sec:data-preprocessing}, we retained individuals with both RNA and ATAC profiles, yielding 335 individuals for Bn cells, 372 for CD4 T cells, and 346 for CD8 T cells. For each individual, we summed counts across cells of the same type to obtain a pseudobulk count for each peak and each gene. We then normalized the counts to counts per million (CPM) and applied the transformation $\ln(1+10^6 C/L)$, where $C$ is the pseudobulk count for a gene or peak and $L$ is the individual's total count across all genes (for RNA) or all peaks (for ATAC) in that cell type.

\noindent For each candidate peak--gene pair, we calculated Spearman's rank correlation across individuals as
\begin{equation*}
\widehat{\rho}_{\mathrm{individual}}
= \operatorname{Cor}_{\mathrm{S}}(\boldsymbol{A}_1,\boldsymbol{E}_1),
\end{equation*}
where $\boldsymbol{A}_1$ and $\boldsymbol{E}_1$ are the log-transformed CPM values for peak accessibility $p$ and gene expression $g$, respectively, ordered by individual. Within each cell type, all pairs had complete observations for the same individuals. We obtained two-sided $P$ values using \texttt{cor.test} in R with \texttt{method="spearman"} and \texttt{exact=FALSE}, which uses a $t$ approximation for $n$ individuals. As described in \nameref{sec:filtering}, we considered pairs with a Benjamini--Hochberg-adjusted $P$ value below 0.1 significant.

\noindent This correlation measures the marginal association between accessibility and expression across individuals within a cell type. Because no covariates were included, it also reflects any factor that varies across donors and affects both measurements, and it is therefore subject to the confounding described in \nameref{S1_Text} (Section~1). Unlike the causal methods, it uses no genetic information.


\subsection{Marginal multiomics}
\label{sec:wnn-correlations}

We used the marginal multiomics method to evaluate marginal peak--gene associations across 80 metacells derived from a publicly available 10x Genomics 10k PBMC Multiome ATAC and Gene Expression dataset \cite{tenx2020pbmc10k}. In the TRIPOD study \cite{jiang2022tripod}, the authors retained 7,790 cells after quality control and annotated them into 14 cell types using labels transferred from a CITE-seq reference \cite{hao2021seurat}. They normalized the RNA data using sctransform \cite{hafemeister2019sctransform} and the ATAC data using term frequency--inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) \cite{stuart2021signac}, then grouped the cells into 80 metacells using weighted nearest-neighbor (WNN) analysis \cite{hao2021seurat}.

\noindent We retained the published metacell assignments. To quantify accessibility at the genomic intervals used in CIMA, we started with the 338,036 CIMA ATAC peaks in hg38, excluded intervals that overlapped another peak in the set, and counted fragments from the 10x dataset over the remaining intervals using Signac's \texttt{FeatureMatrix} \cite{stuart2021signac}. We summed the resulting ATAC counts across cells within each metacell and normalized them to counts per million (CPM) using the total count across retained peaks in that metacell. We then applied a $\log(1+x)$ transformation to both the ATAC CPM values and the supplied RNA values.

\noindent For each candidate peak--gene pair, we calculated Spearman's rank correlation across all 80 metacells as
\begin{equation*}
\widehat{\rho}_{\mathrm{metacell}} =
\operatorname{Cor}_{\mathrm{S}}\left(
\boldsymbol{A}_2,
\boldsymbol{E}_2
\right),
\end{equation*}
where $\boldsymbol{A}_2$ and $\boldsymbol{E}_2$ contain the transformed accessibility values for peak $p$ and expression values for gene $g$, respectively, in the same metacell order. Because the metacells span 14 immune cell types, this correlation reflects differences in accessibility and expression between cell types as well as variation within them. Cell identity can therefore act as a confounder of the within-cell-type relationship (\nameref{S1_Text}, Section~1). The metacells are also not specific to the cell type being analyzed, so a peak--gene pair tested in different cell types has the same correlation estimate and nominal $P$ value.

\subsection{Enrichment for external regulatory references}
\label{sec:hyper}

We tested whether significant peaks identified by each method were enriched for overlap with regulatory regions assigned to the corresponding genes in six external regulatory datasets. FANTOM5 \cite{andersson2014atlas} links enhancers to genes based on enhancer RNA expression. 4DGenome \cite{teng2015fourdgenome} records physical contacts between regulatory regions and genes detected by chromatin interaction assays. FANTOM5 and 4DGenome links were obtained through HACER \cite{wang2019hacer}. EnhancerAtlas~2.0 \cite{gao2020enhanceratlas} integrates enhancer annotations from 12 high-throughput experimental methods, including H3K4me1 and H3K27ac ChIP-seq, DNase-seq, ATAC-seq, and GRO-seq. Whole-blood cis-eQTLs mapped in European-American subjects by the GTEx Consortium \cite{gtex2017genetic} were used to determine whether a peak contained a variant associated with expression of the corresponding gene. These four datasets were used without matching to the three cell types studied here. The remaining two datasets were chromatin contact maps from immune cells: promoter capture Hi-C (PCHi-C) across 17 primary human hematopoietic cell types \cite{javierre2016pchic}, which captures contacts between gene promoters and distal regions, and H3K27ac HiChIP across five common immune cell types \cite{chandra2021pieqtl}, which captures contacts between gene promoters and regions marked by H3K27ac, a histone modification associated with active regulatory elements (Supplementary Table~3 of \cite{chandra2021pieqtl}). For H3K27ac HiChIP, we used interacting regions with $Q<0.1$ in the B-cell, CD4 T-cell, and CD8 T-cell columns; for PCHi-C, we used the non-promoter ends of contacts with a CHiCAGO score $\geq 5$ in Bn cells, CD4 T cells, and CD8 T cells. For GTEx, we used variants with meta-analysis $P<0.05$ and excluded variants located within transcripts annotated in EnsDb.Hsapiens.v86 (version 2.99.0). All regions were lifted over from hg19 to hg38 using rtracklayer (version 1.66.0) \cite{lawrence2009rtracklayer} and the \texttt{hg19ToHg38.over.chain} file.

\noindent The background for each gene $g$ consisted of all its candidate peaks within 1~Mb of its TSS. For a given set of significant peaks and a given regulatory dataset, let $N_g$ denote the number of candidate peaks for $g$, $K_g$ the number of candidate peaks overlapping regulatory regions assigned to $g$, $n_g$ the number of significant peaks for $g$, and $k_g$ the number of significant peaks overlapping those regulatory regions. A gene was included in the hypergeometric test only if the regulatory dataset assigned at least one region to it and $K_g>0$, $n_g>0$, and $N_g>K_g$. We summed the counts across the included genes, yielding $N=\sum_g N_g$, $K=\sum_g K_g$, $n=\sum_g n_g$, and $k=\sum_g k_g$. We then calculated an overall hypergeometric $P$ value as the probability of observing at least $k$ overlapping peaks when drawing $n$ peaks without replacement from $N$ candidate peaks, of which $K$ overlapped the regulatory regions. In R, this was computed using \texttt{phyper(k - 1, K, N - K, n, lower.tail = FALSE, log.p = TRUE)}, which returns the log-transformed $P$ value. The pooled fold enrichment was calculated as $(k/n)/(K/N)$, the overlap proportion among significant peaks divided by the corresponding proportion among candidate peaks.

\noindent Because regulatory annotation coverage varied among genes, we compared overlap proportions between MR-only and background peak--gene pairs within groups of genes with similar coverage. For each dataset, genes were ranked by $K_g/N_g$ and divided into eight groups. Within each group, we calculated the pooled background overlap proportion, $\sum_g K_g/\sum_g N_g$, and the pooled MR-only overlap proportion, $\sum_g k_g/\sum_g n_g$. The 95\% confidence interval for the MR-only proportion was obtained by resampling genes with replacement (2,000 bootstrap resamples). The group-level proportions were plotted against the line $y=x$, which represents overlap at the background rate (Fig.~\ref{fig:go}C and \nameref{S5_Fig}).


\subsection{Gene Ontology analysis}
\label{sec:go}
We performed over-representation analysis with the DAVID Functional Annotation Chart \cite{sherman2022david}. The input was the union of the genes of MR-only links (\nameref{sec:terminology}) across the three cell types, 4,509 gene symbols (OFFICIAL\_GENE\_SYMBOL, \textit{Homo sapiens}, DAVID default background). We used the GOTERM\_BP\_DIRECT and GOTERM\_MF\_DIRECT categories; DAVID exports only terms with FDR $<0.1$. Of the input genes, 4,031 had a GO Biological Process annotation and 4,185 had a GO Molecular Function annotation. Fig~\ref{fig:go}B shows the top 10 terms in each category ranked by DAVID FDR. 


\subsection{Overlap with CIMA eRegulons}
\label{sec:grn}
We compared the links of each method with the CIMA SCENIC+ (1.0.1) enhancer-driven regulons (eRegulons) \cite{yin2026cima,bravo2023scenicplus}. We used two sets: the eRegulons active in the cell type (cell-type eRegulons) and the subset of these that belong to the high-quality eRegulons of the corresponding lineage (high-quality eRegulons). From each set we extracted the unique region--gene pairs. For each cell type and method, we built a $2\times2$ table of all tested pairs in the cell type, cross-classified by whether the pair was significant ($\q<0.05$) and whether it was present in the eRegulon set, and applied Fisher's exact test. We report the odds ratio (Fig~\ref{fig:go}D; \nameref{S8_Fig}).


\subsection{Heritability enrichment analysis}
\label{sec:sldsc}
We used stratified LD score regression (S-LDSC; LDSC v1.0.1) \cite{finucane2015sldsc} to test whether peaks of MR-only links were enriched for disease heritability. The MR-only peaks were the union of the peaks of MR-only links (\nameref{sec:terminology}) across the three cell types. The comparison set was all candidate peaks (316,798 peaks in hg19). Peaks were lifted over from hg38 to hg19 with rtracklayer (1.66.0), keeping only one-to-one mappings on chromosomes 1--22, extended by 500 bp on each side and merged. The annotation was the set of SNPs located in the extended peaks. We fitted S-LDSC separately for the MR-only annotation and for the all-candidate-peak annotation, each together with the 97 baselineLD v2.2 annotations \cite{gazal2017baselineld}, with \texttt{--ld-wind-cm 1}, LD scores restricted to HapMap3 SNPs, and \texttt{--overlap-annot --print-coefficients}. We used the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 European LD reference data and the standard regression weights that exclude the MHC region; the reference files were obtained from Zenodo (doi:10.5281/zenodo.10515792) \cite{gazal2024sldscref}. Enrichment was defined as the proportion of heritability explained by the annotation divided by the proportion of SNPs in the annotation, and we report the enrichment estimate and its $P$ value for the tested annotation.

\noindent We report seven immune-mediated diseases: ankylosing spondylitis (AS), Crohn's disease (CD), inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), rheumatoid arthritis (RA), systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), multiple sclerosis (MS), and type 1 diabetes (T1D). Summary statistics for IBD \cite{delange2017ibd}, RA \cite{saevarsdottir2022ra}, SLE \cite{bentham2015sle} and T1D \cite{chiou2021t1d} were obtained from the collection of 107 independent GWAS summary statistics on Zenodo (\url{https://zenodo.org/records/10515792/files/sumstats_indep107.tgz}) \cite{gazal2024sldscref}. Summary statistics for AS were obtained from the Pan-UK Biobank (\url{https://pan.ukbb.broadinstitute.org/}) \cite{karczewski2025panukb}, those for CD from the International IBD Genetics Consortium (\url{https://www.ibdgenetics.org/}) \cite{liu2015ibd}, and those for MS from ImmunoBase \cite{sawcer2011ms}. The AS and CD summary statistics are those used in the OneK1K study \cite{yazar2022onek1k}. The sample sizes and sources of the seven GWAS are given in \nameref{S4_Table}.


\subsection{Motif enrichment analysis}
\label{sec:motif}
We performed motif enrichment analysis to determine whether transcription factor binding motifs were overrepresented in significant peaks compared with background peaks. For each cell type (Bn cells, CD4 T cells, CD8 T cells) and each of the six methods, we used the peaks of links with $\q<0.1$ as the foreground set. The universe was the peaks of the candidate pairs that were also present in the ATAC object of that cell type (316,117 peaks in Bn cells), and the background was 50,000 peaks drawn from this universe with \texttt{MatchRegionStats}, matched for GC content and $\log(1+\text{total accessibility})$. Motif occurrences were annotated with \texttt{AddMotifs} in Signac (1.14.0) \cite{stuart2021signac} using the JASPAR2020 CORE vertebrate collection (JASPAR2020 0.99.10; latest version of each matrix, 746 motifs) \cite{fornes2020jaspar} and the hg38 genome (BSgenome.Hsapiens.UCSC.hg38 1.4.5). We used \texttt{FindMotifs} in Signac, which computes $P$ values with a hypergeometric test, and adjusted the $P$ values by the Benjamini--Hochberg procedure across motifs within each cell type and method. Motifs with adjusted $P<0.05$ and fold enrichment $>1$ were considered enriched. A motif was MR-specific if it was enriched for at least one of causal IVW, causal Egger and causal SMR and for neither marginal method; causal joint was not used in this definition. For Table~\ref{tab:motif}, we selected for each cell type the two MR-specific motifs with the smallest adjusted $P$ among the causal methods, skipping motifs without a clear literature report.


\subsection{Allele orientation}
\label{sec:orientation}
Genetic variant associations can be coded with respect to either the major or the minor allele. While IVW estimates are invariant to allele orientation, MR-Egger estimates can change. Following standard practice for MR-Egger \cite{bowden2015egger,burgess2017interpreting}, before fitting causal IVW and causal Egger we flipped the sign of $\hat{\gamma}_j$ and $\hat{\Gamma}_j$, together with the corresponding row and column of $\boldsymbol R$, for every SNP with $\hat{\gamma}_j<0$, so that all SNP--exposure associations were positive. Under this convention, directional pleiotropy is defined with respect to the accessibility-increasing allele \cite{bowden2015egger,burgess2017interpreting}. We compared causal Egger with and without this orientation across 176,545 pairs (\nameref{S2_Fig}).


\subsection{Error correlation due to sample overlap}
\label{sec:overlap}
Two-sample MR typically uses SNP--exposure and SNP--outcome associations from independent samples \cite{burgess2016overlap}, whereas our caQTL and eQTL summary statistics were both derived from the CIMA cohort (sample sizes in \nameref{S3_Table}). Most individuals therefore contributed to both analyses, and we treat our setting as a one-sample design, in which the bias due to sample overlap is largest. In \nameref{S1_Text} (Section~4), we show that sample overlap affects the IVW estimate only through the correlation $\rho_j$ between the estimation errors of $\hat{\gamma}_j$ and $\hat{\Gamma}_j$, and only to the extent that the NOME assumption is violated. To first order, the resulting bias is of order $1/\bar{F}$, where $\bar{F}$ is the average caQTL $\chi^2$ statistic of the instruments; for our instruments, all caQTL $\chi^2$ statistics exceeded 32.8 (nominal $P<10^{-8}$). To assess the magnitude of $\rho_j$ empirically, we estimated it from SNPs with no detectable association with either trait (null SNPs), separately for each cell type.

\noindent We first performed LD pruning (\texttt{--indep-pairwise 500kb 50 0.1}, MAF $\geq 0.01$, with MAF computed in the reference panel) on the caQTL variants on chromosomes 1--22 that matched the EAS reference panel. For each peak--gene pair, we then retained the pruned SNPs with nominal $P>0.05$ in both the caQTL and eQTL analyses as null SNPs and computed their $z$-scores,
\begin{equation*}
z_{Xj} = \frac{\hat{\gamma}_j}{s_{Xj}}, \qquad z_{Yj} = \frac{\hat{\Gamma}_j}{s_{Yj}}.
\end{equation*}
For a SNP with no effect on either trait, $\operatorname{Corr}(z_{Xj},z_{Yj})=\rho_j$ (\nameref{S1_Text}, Section~4), so the correlation of the two $z$-scores across null SNPs estimates the error correlation. Because $\rho_j$ depends on the SNP only through the sample sizes, this estimate also applies, to first order, to the instruments of the same pair.

\noindent We estimated this correlation in two ways. First, for each cell type, we pooled all (pair, SNP) observations: 17,074,938 (Bn cells), 33,263,695 (CD4 T cells), and 21,537,822 (CD8 T cells) observations. Because the same SNP can appear in several pairs, these observations are not independent. Second, we calculated the correlation separately for each peak--gene pair with more than two null SNPs (547,452, 1,051,953, and 686,829 pairs, respectively) and tested it with a $t$ test with $n-2$ degrees of freedom, where $n$ is the number of null SNPs in the pair. In this analysis, alleles were aligned by flipping the sign of the eQTL slope for variants with reversed alleles, rather than by removing them as in the main MR analysis. The distributions of the pair-specific correlations and $P$ values are shown in \nameref{S1_Fig}.
